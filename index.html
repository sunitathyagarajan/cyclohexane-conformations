Cyclohexane Conformations

Cyclohexane is the most common ring in organic chemistry, and it is not flat. In this module you will handle 3D models of its chair and boat forms, watch one chair flip into the other, and put numbers on why the chair wins.

Part 1

Why isn't cyclohexane flat?

An sp³ carbon wants its bonds about 109.5° apart. A flat regular hexagon forces every C–C–C angle to 120°, so a planar cyclohexane would carry angle strain at all six carbons. Worse, in a flat ring every pair of neighboring C–H bonds lines up exactly, so all six C–C bonds would be eclipsed, the same torsional strain that makes eclipsed ethane 2.9 kcal/mol (12 kJ/mol) less stable than staggered ethane.

The ring escapes both problems by puckering. Alternate carbons move above and below the mean plane. Bond angles close to about 111° and every C–C bond becomes staggered. Measured by heat of combustion, cyclohexane has essentially zero ring strain.

Part 2

The chair and the boat

Two puckered shapes keep the bond angles near tetrahedral. In the chair, carbons alternate up and down all the way around the ring. In the boat, two carbons opposite each other (C1 and C4 here) both point up, like the bow and stern of a boat.

Drawing tip: axial and equatorial

Axial bonds are vertical and alternate up, down, up around the ring. An "up" carbon (one that sits above the mean plane) has its axial bond pointing up. Equatorial bonds point outward and are drawn parallel to the ring bonds one bond removed: the equatorial bond on C1 runs parallel to C2–C3 and C5–C6. Every carbon has exactly one of each.

Part 3

The ring flip: one chair becomes another

At room temperature cyclohexane does not sit still. It interconverts between two chair forms about 105 times per second by rotating around its C–C bonds; no bonds break. The path climbs through a half-chair transition state, drops into a twist-boat intermediate, passes over the boat, and comes back down through a second half-chair to the other chair.

Chair

Up stays up. A ring flip turns every axial position into an equatorial one and every equatorial position into an axial one. It does not move a group from the top face of the ring to the bottom face. Follow any orange hydrogen: it starts axial and pointing up, and ends equatorial but still on the top face. This is why cis and trans relationships in substituted cyclohexanes survive a ring flip.

Turn on the methyl group and run the flip. The methyl starts equatorial. After the flip it is axial, and the dotted lines show it crowding the axial hydrogens on C3 and C5. That is the 1,3-diaxial interaction you will put a number on in Part 4.

Part 4

Thermodynamics: how much does the chair win by?

Energies along the ring-flip pathway

ConformationRole on the pathwaykcal/molkJ/molMain source of strain

Twist-boatLocal minimum (intermediate)5.523Partial eclipsing, reduced flagpole contact
BoatTransition state between twist-boats6.929Two eclipsed C–C bonds, flagpole H···H clash
Half-chairTransition state for the ring flip10.845Angle strain plus near-eclipsing

An energy difference becomes a population ratio through the relationship between the standard free-energy change and the equilibrium constant:

ΔG° = −RT ln K   ⇔   K = e−ΔG°/RT

Here R = 1.987 cal mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ (8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹) and T is in kelvin. At 298 K, RT = 0.592 kcal/mol, so every 1.36 kcal/mol of energy difference changes K by a factor of 10.

For the chair ⇌ twist-boat equilibrium, ΔG° ≈ +5.5 kcal/mol gives K ≈ 1 × 10−4. Roughly one molecule in ten thousand is a twist-boat at any moment; the rest are chairs. The boat itself is a transition state, so essentially no molecules sit in it.

Substituents

A-values and 1,3-diaxial strain

Put one substituent on cyclohexane and the two chairs are no longer equal. In one chair the group is equatorial; after a ring flip it is axial. The A-value of a substituent is the free-energy difference between those two chairs:

A = G°(axial) − G°(equatorial) = −ΔG° for axial ⇌ equatorial

A positive A-value means the equatorial chair is lower in energy. The larger the A-value, the more strongly the group prefers to be equatorial. Because it is a free energy, you can turn it straight into a population with K = eA/RT, where K = [equatorial]/[axial].

Where the energy comes from. An axial group on C1 points straight along the ring axis, parallel to the axial hydrogens on C3 and C5 on the same face. In cyclohexane itself, those three axial hydrogens are only about 2.5 Å apart, so anything bigger than H in that spot gets crowded. The crowding is called a 1,3-diaxial interaction. Look down the C1–C2 bond and you will see why: an axial group is gauche (60°) to C3 of the ring, just like the two methyls in gauche butane. An equatorial group is anti (180°) to C3. An axial methyl has two such gauche relationships (one with C3, one with C5) at about 0.9 kcal/mol each, which predicts 1.8 kcal/mol. The measured A-value of methyl is 1.74 kcal/mol (7.3 kJ/mol).

How it is measured. At room temperature the ring flips too fast to see both chairs. Below about −80 °C the flip slows enough that ¹H or ¹³C NMR shows separate signals for the axial and equatorial conformers. Integrating them gives K, and A = RT ln K.

Values are typical literature figures and vary by a few tenths of a kcal/mol with solvent and measurement method. Notice that A-values follow how much of the group sits over the ring near C1, not how heavy the group is.

Using A-values for disubstituted rings

To compare the two chairs of a disubstituted cyclohexane, add up the A-values of the groups that are axial in each chair. The chair with the smaller total wins. In cis-1-tert-butyl-4-methylcyclohexane, one chair has t-Bu axial (about 4.9 kcal/mol) and the other has CH₃ axial (1.74 kcal/mol). The second chair is favored by about 3.2 kcal/mol, so more than 99% of molecules keep t-Bu equatorial even though that forces the methyl axial. This shortcut works when the two groups do not touch each other. Two groups that are both axial on the same face at C1 and C3 clash much more than their A-values suggest.

How fast does the ring flip?

The rate comes from the barrier, not from ΔG°. The Eyring equation turns the half-chair barrier (ΔG‡ ≈ 10.8 kcal/mol) into a rate constant at the temperature you set:

k = (kBT / h) · e−ΔG‡/RT

Average time per flip (1/k)

  • Chair is the ground state. Near-tetrahedral angles and all-staggered bonds give it essentially no strain.
  • Boat costs 6.9 kcal/mol. Two eclipsed bonds and the flagpole clash. Twisting relieves part of this, so the twist-boat (5.5) is the real intermediate.
  • The flip barrier is 10.8 kcal/mol. It is low enough for about 105 flips per second at 25 °C, and slow enough to freeze out near −100 °C.
  • Flipping swaps axial and equatorial. Up stays up. Bulky groups prefer equatorial by their A-value.
